0:00 – 0:15

The spinner that lies

In Lecture 1 you judged Patch A, which disables the Reserve button while a request is on its way. It did nothing for Maya and Sam racing on two phones, so we did not ship it. Patch A was aimed at a different problem. Today we look at that problem from Maya's side of the screen.

It is a slow payment day: the payment provider takes 3 seconds to answer instead of 0.3. Maya opens AfterHours, types her name and presses Reserve on the escape room, which has one place left.

Nothing near the button changes. A small spinner turns in the page header, next to the title. After a second and a half, Maya presses Reserve again.

Predict (1 minute, show of hands)
  1. What is the last message Maya sees?
  2. How many places does she hold when she walks away?

Follow it in order. The first request reserved the last place as #228, and the page said You're in! The second request, from the second tap, re-checked a moment later, found the room full and got an HTTP 409. The page replaced the good news with Sorry, that event just filled up. A few seconds later that message disappeared too.

Every message was accurate about the request it answered. Maya still walks away believing she has no place, while the database says she holds the last one. On an event with room, the same two taps book her twice: #229 and #230 in our run.

Now look at the spinner. It stopped at 3.0 seconds, when the first answer arrived, while the second request was still running. The page's only sign of activity switched off with work still in flight. That is the spinner that lies.

About this page

We planted this page's problems on purpose, for the lab. The slow payment is a setting (AFTERHOURS_PAYMENT_DELAY=3); the reservation code underneath is the Week 1 version with Patch B, and it did its job both times.

Key idea

Running without errors is not the same as usable. The system state is what the database holds. The user belief is what the person thinks happened. A system that answers every request correctly can still leave a person believing the wrong thing, and they act on the belief.

The kit's test suite says 9 passed. No test taps twice, waits three seconds, or reads the last message on the screen. An agent asked to "make reservations work" can finish with every test green without ever seeing the page. This week's evidence has to come from people: watching them use the page, and checking it the way they use it.

0:15 – 0:35

Watch people, don't coach

In the engineering loop from Lecture 1, this week is the arrow from Observe back to Understand. What people actually do with the product changes what you believe they need, and that belief feeds the next specification.

A usability test gives a real person a realistic task, and you watch them do it. It answers questions a demo cannot: where do people look first, what do they expect a button to do, and where do they hesitate. Three rules make the session worth running.

  • Give a goal, not steps. "You and your friend Lee want to go to Board Game Night. Get both of you a place." Steps such as "type your name and press Reserve" tell the participant where to look. A goal lets you see whether they find it.
  • Ask them to think aloud. They say what they look at, what they expect and what puzzles them, as they go. Say at the start that you are testing the page, not them.
  • Don't coach. When they get stuck, wait. Use only neutral prompts, such as "What are you thinking?" or "What do you expect to happen?". The moment you point or explain, you are measuring your help instead of the design.

Write down what you see, with times, rather than your interpretation: "pressed Reserve twice, about 1.5 s apart; said 'did it go through?'" is evidence. "Found it confusing" is an opinion about the evidence.

Neutral or coaching? (pairs, 5 minutes)

You are facilitating. Mark each line N (neutral, fine to say) or C (coaching or leading).

  1. "Take your time. What are you looking for?"
  2. "Try the orange button."
  3. "What do you expect to happen when you press that?"
  4. "Is the Reserve button easy to find?"
  5. "Great, you did that right."
  6. "What are you thinking right now?"

Then write one task for an AfterHours participant: a goal, a reason to care, and how they will know they are done.

Debrief: neutral or coaching

Neutral: 1, 3 and 6. They ask about the participant's thinking and expectations without pointing anywhere. Line 3 is fine once the participant has chosen something to press themselves.

Coaching: 2 points at the answer. 4 names the button the participant has not found yet, and invites a polite "yes". 5 tells them they succeeded, so they stop checking, and it teaches them what you hoped they would do. Praise the effort at the end of the session, not the steps during it.

A good task: "The festival opens on Friday. You and your friend Lee want to go to Board Game Night together. Get a place for both of you, then tell me how you know you both have one." The last sentence gives you a clean end point and makes the participant say what they believe, which you can compare with who_booked.py.

How many people?

The most quoted answer is five. It comes from Jakob Nielsen (2000), building on a model he published with Tom Landauer in 1993. Suppose each test user runs into a given problem with probability L. Then n users find a share 1 − (1 − L)n of the problems. Averaged across the projects they studied, L was 31%, and at that value five users find about 85% of the problems (the formula gives 84%).

Read the claim with its own caveats. Nielsen's actual advice was to spend a budget of 15 users on three rounds of five, fixing what you find between rounds. When two quite different groups use a product, he suggested three or four users from each. In a 2006 follow-up, he recommended about 20 users when you need numbers, such as task times, rather than problems. And L is an average: a problem that only screen-reader users or only first-time visitors meet has a much lower L for a general group. Laura Faulkner (2003) tested 60 users and drew random groups of five from them. Some groups found 99% of the problems; others found only 55%.

Key idea

Five users is a budget for one round, with one kind of user, looking for the big problems. It is not evidence that everything else is fine. If you need to know about keyboard or screen-reader users, test with them or use the procedures in the next section.

This week's homework is one unaided session with another team (about 1.5 hours; the script and note sheet are in the lab kit).

Keyboard, labels, focus, contrast

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are the W3C's standard for accessible web content. Version 2.2 became a W3C Recommendation on 5 October 2023 and was republished with corrections on 12 December 2024. Its testable success criteria sit under four principles:

PrincipleIn plain wordsOn the AfterHours page
PerceivablePeople can perceive the content, whatever senses they use.Text contrast is high enough; the result of a reservation reaches a screen-reader user.
OperableEvery function works, whatever input people use.Reserve works from a keyboard; you can see where the focus is.
UnderstandablePeople can understand the content and how to use it.The name field says what to type; an error says what went wrong and what to do.
RobustAssistive technology can read it reliably.Controls expose their name, role and state: a real <button>, a real label.

Each criterion has a level: A, AA or AAA. The laws in reading R2 all point at level AA.

Two mechanisms you need first
  1. The accessibility tree. The browser turns your HTML into pixels for the screen and into an accessibility tree for assistive technology. Each node has a role (button, text field, heading), a name (what it is called) and states (unavailable, invalid). A screen reader reads the tree, not the pixels. The name comes from the element's text, from its <label>, or from attributes such as aria-label.
  2. Keyboard focus. Only some elements can take focus: links, form fields, buttons, and anything given tabindex. Tab moves focus through them in document order. The browser draws a focus indicator around the focused element, unless the page's CSS removes it.

With those two in mind, here are the six problems planted in this week's kit. Each has a mechanism you can now name.

Answer: the Tab path, before and after

On the kit, the only stop is the name field, and its name is the example text. Tab never lands on a Reserve control, so a keyboard user cannot book anything. After the two changes there are six stops: the field, now called "Your name", then one button per event. The fixed page also adds hidden words to each button, so a screen-reader user who lists the buttons hears "Reserve a place at Board Game Night" rather than five identical "Reserve"s.

There is a catch. Once Reserve is a real button, a keyboard user who books a place finds focus thrown back to the page itself, because the page rebuilds every card after each reservation and destroys the focused button. That problem has been in AfterHours since Week 1; the <div>s hid it. Fixing one problem often uncovers the next.

Contrast, computed

Contrast is a ratio between two colours' relative luminance, a measure of brightness from 0 (black) to 1 (white). WCAG 2.2 defines it exactly: turn each red, green and blue value into a linear light value, weight them 0.2126, 0.7152 and 0.0722 (your eyes are most sensitive to green), then take (Llighter + 0.05) / (Ldarker + 0.05). The result runs from 1:1 to 21:1. Normal text needs at least 4.5:1. Large text (at least 18 point, or 14 point bold) and the visible parts of controls, such as a field's border or a focus ring, need 3:1.

For the kit's time and venue line, #625b8a on #1f1a3d, the luminances are 0.119 and 0.0137, so the ratio is (0.119 + 0.05) / (0.0137 + 0.05) = 2.66. The kit ships scripts/contrast.py so you can check any pair yourself.

Live regions

A screen reader reads what has focus, or what the user moves to. A message that appears somewhere else on the page is silent unless it is in a live region: an element whose changes the screen reader announces without moving focus. role="status" makes a polite one, announced when the screen reader finishes what it is saying. The region has to exist before the message arrives. MDN's advice is to put it, empty, in the page's initial markup, then change its text. That is why Week 1's toast, which had role="status" but was hidden until it appeared together with its text, was a weak design even before this week's kit removed the role.

Manual procedures, named combinations

None of this shows up in a test run. The kit's 9 passing tests say nothing about focus, names, contrast or announcements, and 900 backend tests would say nothing either: they send HTTP requests and never press Tab or listen. In the lab you check each problem with a manual procedure on a named combination:

  • Keyboard only in Chrome or Firefox (127 or newer), on any system.
  • VoiceOver with Safari on macOS (built in; Command-F5 turns it on).
  • NVDA with Firefox on Windows (NVDA is free and open source).

Name the combination in your evidence. Screen readers and browsers differ, so "it works" means "it works on this combination". In WebAIM's survey of 1,539 screen-reader users (December 2023 to January 2024), the most common pairings were JAWS with Chrome (24.7%) and NVDA with Chrome (21.3%); NVDA with Firefox was 10.0% and VoiceOver with Safari 7.0%. We support the two free combinations. That is a teaching choice, and it covers a minority of real users.

0:55 – 1:10

Design every state

A request can end in more ways than "success" and "error". List the situations a person can be in after pressing Reserve:

  • Ready: nothing is happening yet.
  • Reserving: the request is on its way.
  • Still reserving: the wait is longer than usual.
  • Reserved: HTTP 201, with a reservation number.
  • Refused: HTTP 409, the event filled up.
  • Name missing: the page caught it before sending.
  • Not sent: the device is offline, so nothing left it.
  • Unknown: the connection failed after the request left, or the server answered with a 5xx error. The page cannot know whether the server saved a reservation.

Designing a state means deciding four things for it: what the button shows, what the message says, what a screen reader hears, and where keyboard focus is. The same list works for the programme itself: loading, loaded, empty, and failed to load.

One more limit: disabling the button, or ignoring a second press as the fixed page does, stops this page from sending twice. It does nothing about a second tab or a network retry. The page's guard is a courtesy to the user; the server's key is the guarantee.

1:10 – 1:25

How a wait feels

How long can a wait be before it hurts? The most cited answer is Jakob Nielsen's three limits (1993), which he drew from earlier research by Robert Miller (1968) and by Stuart Card, George Robertson and Jock Mackinlay (1991):

  • 0.1 second is about the limit for the system to feel as if it reacts instantaneously. No special feedback is needed beyond showing the result.
  • 1 second is about the limit for the user's flow of thought to stay uninterrupted, although they notice the delay.
  • 10 seconds is about the limit for keeping their attention on the task. Longer operations need a percent-done indicator, and his 2014 update adds a clearly signposted way to interrupt.

He adds that feedback during a delay matters most when the response time is likely to vary a lot. A payment check that usually takes 0.3 s and sometimes 3 s is exactly that case.

Four habits turn the limits into design:

  • Acknowledge within 0.1 s, where the person is looking. The fixed page changes the button's own text within 0.05 s of the tap. The kit's spinner was fast too, but in the header, far from the finger.
  • For waits over a second, say what is happening. Nielsen Norman Group's guidance (Sherwin, 2014) is a looped animation for waits of about 2 to 10 seconds and a percent-done indicator for 10 seconds or more.
  • Don't pretend. A fake progress bar, or showing "You're in!" before the server confirms (so-called optimistic UI), makes a wait feel shorter. For a "like" that is fine. For a reservation it is a promise the server might not keep: the spinner that lies, the other way round.
  • Make it actually faster when the wait is yours to fix. Measure first (Week 11).
Pick the feedback (pairs, 4 minutes)

For each wait, choose what the page shows and when: nothing special, a change on the control, a looped animation with words, or a percent-done indicator with a way to cancel.

  1. Loading the programme on crowded festival Wi-Fi: suppose about 2 seconds.
  2. Reserving: usually 0.3 s, sometimes 3 s.
  3. An organiser exporting all 2,000 reservations as a spreadsheet: suppose about 20 seconds.
Debrief: feedback for three waits
  • Programme, about 2 s: show where the list will be ("Loading the programme…") with a looped animation, and keep the last good list if a refresh fails. Tell a screen reader the area is busy (aria-busy) rather than announcing every card.
  • Reserving, 0.3 to 3 s: change the button within 0.1 s every time, because you cannot know in advance which wait this will be. After about 2 s, add words that say only what the page knows: still waiting for confirmation, no need to press again.
  • Export, about 20 s: beyond the attention limit. A percent-done indicator (the server knows how many rows it has written), a way to cancel, and ideally "we'll email it when it's ready" so the organiser can leave.

Beyond the booking page

Four short readings. The first two widen the view: who gains from accessible design, and what the law asks. The last two go deeper into the craft: writing error messages, and knowing what each kind of check can see. Read them before the lab.

R1

The curb-cut effect

A curb cut is the small ramp where a pavement meets the road. Disabled people fought for them. One evening in the early 1970s, activists in Berkeley, California, poured cement into a crude ramp at a curb; in 1972, pressed by disabled activists, the city installed its first official curb cut on Telegraph Avenue. The idea was older. In Kalamazoo, Michigan, test ramps were built in 1945, and a March 1946 letter to the mayor, quoted in Steven Brown's 1999 history of those ramps, says they gave disabled veterans, disabled non-veterans, aged and infirm persons and mothers with baby carriages more freedom of movement.

In a 2017 essay, Angela Glover Blackwell gave the pattern a name. Laws and programmes designed for vulnerable groups, she argued, often end up benefiting everyone: I like to think of it as the 'curb-cut effect'. When the curbs came down, parents with strollers, workers pushing heavy carts, travellers with luggage, runners and skateboarders all headed for the ramps.

Software has its own curb cuts. Keyboard access helps power users and anyone whose trackpad has just died. Captions help people on a noisy train. A clear message next to the button, saying what is happening, helps everyone on crowded festival Wi-Fi: the fix for K6 is for Maya, whoever she is.

A caveat worth keeping

The curb-cut effect is a reason to expect wider benefits. It is not a condition for doing the work. Access is owed to disabled people whether or not anyone else gains from it.

R2

The law differs by country

Many countries have accessibility laws, and many of them point to WCAG, often an older version, as the technical yardstick. The details differ by country, by sector and over time. Three examples, checked in September 2026 (this is not legal advice):

  • United States, federal agencies. The Section 508 standards incorporate WCAG 2.0 Level A and AA, and have applied since 18 January 2018.
  • United States, state and local government. A 2024 Department of Justice rule under the Americans with Disabilities Act (Title II) adopted WCAG 2.1 Level AA. In April 2026 the department pushed the compliance dates back by a year, to 26 April 2027 for larger public entities and 26 April 2028 for smaller ones.
  • European Union. The European Accessibility Act (Directive 2019/882) applies from 28 June 2025 to many products and services sold to consumers, including e-commerce; microenterprises providing services are exempt. The European standard EN 301 549 v3.2.1, based on WCAG 2.1 AA, remains the reference. Version 4.1.1, aligned with WCAG 2.2, was published in September 2026 and is not yet cited in the EU's Official Journal.

Two practical lessons. First, the rules move: the US dates above changed this year, and the EU's reference standard is changing. Check the current rules for where your product is used before you rely on them. Second, WCAG 2.2 keeps the 2.0 and 2.1 criteria essentially unchanged, with one exception (4.1.1 Parsing, removed as obsolete), so building to 2.2 AA is a sound engineering target even where the law names an older version.

R3

Writing error messages

An error message is a state you designed, or one you left to chance. WCAG 2.2 sets a minimum: if an input error is detected automatically, the item in error is identified and the error is described in text (3.3.1, level A), and where a fix is known, it is suggested (3.3.3, level AA). A good message does more than that. It answers three questions in the person's words: what happened, what it means for them, and what to do next.

  • Use the person's words. "HTTP 500" and "invalid input" describe the system. "Type your name first, then press Reserve" describes the task.
  • Make the next step safe. Only suggest trying again if trying again cannot do harm.
  • Put it where they are looking, and leave it there. Next to the field or the button, not in a corner; not gone after 3.5 seconds; and in a live region, so a screen-reader user hears it (4.1.3).
  • Don't blame, and don't lose their work. Keep what they typed. Move focus to the field that needs fixing, and link the message to the field so a screen reader reads both.
R4

What each check can see

Automated accessibility checkers read a page's structure and computed styles and apply rules to them. They are cheap and fast, and they find a lot. Each year WebAIM runs its checker over the home pages of the top million websites. In the February 2026 run, 95.9% of home pages had detected WCAG 2 failures; low-contrast text was the most common, on 83.9% of pages, and 51% of pages had form inputs with no label. WebAIM also warns: Absence of detected errors does not indicate that a page is accessible or conformant.

We ran one such checker, axe-core 4.13, on the kit page in Chrome. It flagged the low-contrast text (K4) and nothing else. It accepted the placeholder as the field's name (K2). It cannot tell that a <div> with a click listener is meant to be a button (K1). It does not press Tab (K3, K7), wait for an announcement (K5) or tap twice (K6).

Use each check for what it can see. Tests guard the promises the server keeps. Checkers give cheap, early warnings on every change. An agent is a fast first reader with good suggestions. People on named combinations, and people who have never seen your page, tell you what it is like to use it.
