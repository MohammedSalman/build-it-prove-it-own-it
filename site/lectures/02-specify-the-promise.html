Week 2 · Requirements when code is cheap

Discover the Problem, Specify the Promise

We gave two coding agents the same one-line request. Both wrote careful, tested code, and both left the most important decision unmade. This lecture is about finding the decisions hidden in a request, learning from real people which way to make them, and writing them down so that nobody, human or agent, has to guess.

90-minute lecture 2-hour lab ~30 minutes of reading after class Loop: Specify (from Understand) Kit: two real agent waitlists

By the end of today you can

  • Find the product decisions hidden in a one-line request, and say who should make each one.
  • Compare two implementations of the same request by what they do, not by how they look.

  • Interview without leading: ask about past behaviour, not opinions of your idea.

Key idea

Ambiguity is a product decision waiting to happen. A coding agent turns a vague request into confident, tested code. Where the request is silent, the agent either guesses or leaves a gap, and the code's default quietly becomes the policy. Two agents can guess differently in the details and still both skip the decision that matters most.

About this experiment

We ran each agent once, on one day, with the same text. A second run would likely differ in details. Two runs say nothing general about which tool is better, and that was never the question. Both results are kept verbatim in the lab kit with their provenance, including Agent A's full event log.

0:12 – 0:25

What does fair mean?

"People can join a waitlist" hides two separate decisions. The first is who gets the places when booking opens and demand is far higher than supply. The second is who gets a place that frees up later. Most people's first answer to both is "it should be fair". The trouble is that several different rules are all fair in some sense, and they pick different winners.

  • First come, first served. The earliest requests win. Simple to explain and to build. AfterHours does nearly this today: a place goes to the first request that gets through payment and is saved, so a slow payment can lose to a later click. It rewards being fast: a quick connection, a free timetable at the moment booking opens, a page you can scan by eye.
  • Lottery. Everyone who applies within a window (say 30 minutes) goes into a draw. Speed stops mattering; everyone in the window has the same chance. It needs a deadline, a draw, and a way to tell people the result.
  • Quota. Some places are kept for a group, for example first-years who don't yet know how the festival works. Inside each group you still need a rule, such as first come, first served.

Here is one made-up crowd: eight students want the last four places of an event, and booking opens at 12:00. Our script who_wins.py (in the lab kit) applies each policy to exactly this crowd.

The quota guaranteed two first-years a place and still left Omar out. Each rule is a defensible answer to a different question about what the festival is for.

The second decision: a place frees up

Now the question both agents skipped. On Friday at 14:00 someone cancels. Sam, Ana and Zoe are waiting, in that order. Lee is not waiting, but he happens to open the page at 14:02. Four possible policies:

  • Open to all. Nobody is told; whoever completes a booking first wins. This is what both agents' apps do.
  • Auto-promote. Number 1 gets the place at once, and the payment is authorised without asking.
  • Timed offer. Number 1 is offered the place for a fixed time (here 60 minutes). If the offer runs out, that person moves to the end of the list and the offer goes to the new number 1.
  • Lottery among the waiting. One name is drawn from the waitlist.
Authored, not generated

Auto-promote, timed offer and lottery were written by the course staff for this lecture. Neither agent built any of them. We added them because the real outputs don't disagree on this question: both skipped it.

In pairs (5 minutes)

You are the festival committee. Pick one policy for places that free up at the escape room. Write down who it helps, who it hurts, and one thing you would have to build for it (a notification? a clock? consent to charge later?). You will turn it into examples at 0:45.

Key idea

Fair names a choice about who wins. Code can carry that choice out, but it should not make it. A named person makes it, and the spec writes it down where everyone, including the next agent, can read it.

0:25 – 0:45

Discovery: ask about the past

You can't choose between those policies from a desk. Whether a timed offer should last 30 minutes or 3 hours depends on how quickly real students read messages. Whether a quota matters depends on whether first-years really miss out. That is what the Understand step is for: talking to the people the software is for.

Talking to people is easy to do badly. Ask "Would you use a waitlist?" and almost everyone says yes. They are being kind, and they are guessing about a future they haven't lived. Ask "What did you do the last time an event you wanted was full?" and you get a story with facts in it: times, numbers, workarounds. Facts about what people did are much harder to bend than opinions about your idea.

Rob Fitzpatrick's book The Mom Test (2013) sums this up in three rules: Talk about their life instead of your idea; Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future; Talk less and listen more.

Leading, hypothetical, or opinionAbout the past
"Would you use a waitlist?""Tell me about the last time something you wanted to go to was full. What did you do?"
"Wouldn't it be great to get a message when a place frees up?""How did you find out about the last change to an event you were going to?"
"Is first come, first served fair?""Last time tickets for something went fast, who got them? What did people say afterwards?"
"How long would you need to accept an offer?""When did you last miss a message you needed? Where were you?"

The best questions are often the follow-ups: What happened next? When was that? How many times? Can you show me?

0:45 – 1:05

From need to spec

A specification is a ladder from what someone asked for to something a machine can check. Each rung pins down decisions that the rung below leaves open. Where a rung is missing, whoever writes the code, person or agent, has to guess.

Climb it once with the waitlist, using the policy a pair chose at 0:12, a 60-minute timed offer.

1 · User story. Who wants what, and why. The usual template is "As a …, I want …, so that …", usually credited to a team at the company Connextra in the early 2000s.

As a student who missed out on a full event, I want a freed place to be offered to me in the order I joined, so that I don't have to keep refreshing the page.

A story is a reminder of a conversation. It says nothing yet about how long an offer lasts or what Lee sees at 14:02.

2 · Acceptance examples. Concrete cases with names and numbers, written as Given (the starting situation), When (one action), Then (what must be true afterwards). This form comes from behaviour-driven development, where Daniel Terhorst-North and Chris Matts developed it.

Given the workshop is full and Sam is number 1 on its waitlist
When  Maya cancels at 14:00
Then  Sam is offered her place until 15:00
And   Lee, who is not waiting, still sees "Full"

Given Sam's offer has not been answered
When  15:00 passes
Then  the offer moves to Ana until 16:00
And   the waitlist is now Ana, Zoe, Sam

Given Ana's offer has not been answered either
When  16:00 passes
Then  the offer moves to Zoe until 17:00
And   the waitlist is now Zoe, Sam, Ana

The second example forced a decision the story didn't mention: does Sam lose his spot when an offer runs out? We said he moves to the end of the list. The third checks that the rule still works when it happens twice in a row: nobody is skipped for ever, and nobody jumps ahead. You might decide instead that a missed offer removes you. Either way, it is now written down.

3 · Rules table. One row per situation, and every row gets an outcome. Tables make missing cases visible: an empty cell is a question.

4 · Quality requirements. Also called non-functional requirements: how well the feature must work, and for whom. Write them as checkable examples too.

  • Usability: a first-year who has never used AfterHours joins the workshop waitlist on a phone and can say her position within 60 seconds, without help (3 of 3 test users).
  • Accessibility: a screen reader user joins using only the keyboard, hears her position, and can find it again after the message has gone. Reading R3 ties this to WCAG 2.2.
  • Privacy: other students see how many people are waiting, never their names. The waitlist is deleted after the event.

5 · Tests. Each example becomes a test that talks to the app the way a user would. That is the next section.

1:05 – 1:20

The spec divergence test

An acceptance example is a question you can put to any implementation. The lab kit's scripts/compare.py does exactly that: it copies an examples file into a scratch copy of each app, runs it, and prints which example passes where. The examples talk to each app through its HTTP API, so the same example can judge all three apps. Two teaching adapters bend that rule, and they only work because all three share Week 1's code: free_a_place deletes a reservation row, because no app can cancel yet (so it also skips anything a real cancel feature would trigger), and one example watches the shared payment module.

The kit gives you two examples. Each one encodes a decision the course staff made for this exercise; another team could decide the opposite.

def test_example_1_someone_with_a_place_cannot_also_wait_for_one(client):
    """
    Given Maya has just taken the last place at the escape room
    When  Maya presses "Join waitlist" for the escape room
    Then  she is told she already has a place (HTTP 409 Conflict)
    And   the escape room's waitlist stays empty
    """
    escape_room = event_by_title(client, ESCAPE_ROOM)
    assert reserve(client, escape_room, "Maya").status_code == 201

    response = join_waitlist(client, escape_room, "Maya")

    assert response.status_code == 409, f"got {response.status_code}: {response.json()}"
    assert waiting_count(client, escape_room) == 0

Read the table by pattern, not row by row.

  • Every example fails on base. Good: they all test the waitlist. An example that passes on an app without the feature tests something else.
  • Examples 1 to 3 split the agents. Each is a detail one agent decided one way and the other the opposite way, silently. Example 2 is the only one where B's choice matches ours.
  • Examples 4 and 5 pass on both. Agreement is reassuring, but it is not proof of correctness. Both agents also agreed on first come, first served, and nobody chose that either.
  • Examples 6 to 8 fail everywhere. Failing everywhere means: investigate. Here we did. No code, README, test or report in either app states a rule for these cases, and each example says exactly what the class decided. So these are decisions nobody had made until the class made them.

One more habit from Week 1: check the claims. Agent B's report said that when it weakened its transaction, its test of four people joining at once failed. We removed the transaction and ran B's tests 20 times: that test passed all 20 times, and a different test (the same person joining twice at once) failed 6 times. The transaction matters, but the evidence B cited is not what shows it.

Key idea

An example that fails on every implementation is a question to investigate. It can mean a bug in all of them, a wrong example, or, as with examples 6 to 8 here, a decision nobody had made. Examples that split two implementations show where the request allowed more than one reading. Write examples before you delegate, and each of those decisions has an owner instead of a default.

1:20 – 1:25

The one-page scope agreement

Everything from today fits on one page that the team agrees to before anyone, human or agent, writes the feature. It says what is in, what is out for now, the rules, the examples that prove them, the quality requirements, the questions still open with who answers them by when, and one named owner.

Two rules make the page useful. Out is as important as In: saying "not in this release" is a decision, and it stops an agent from helpfully building it anyway. Open questions are allowed, but each one has a person and a date. In the lab you write this page for your own team's product.

ADMIT ONE · WEEK 3

After class · about 30 minutes

Decisions, evidence, and quality

Four short readings. The first two are about what an unmade decision costs, and how to check a famous claim about that cost. The last two are about writing requirements that people and agents can both use.

R1

The cost of a missing decision

A decision nobody makes still gets made. It gets made later, by someone with less time and less information: by the code's default, by a teammate fixing a bug late at night, by a volunteer at the door at 20:05, or by the next agent that touches the file.

A missing decision also spreads. Each agent built on top of its guess. Agent A's own test test_positions_and_retries_survive_app_restart expects a repeated join to answer 201. Agent B's test_joining_twice_keeps_your_place_in_line expects 200. The page text, the API documentation in A's README and B's commit message all repeat the same guesses. If the committee now decides the other way, every one of those changes too, and the tests that used to protect the code now defend the guess.

The cheapest moment to decide is while someone is asking, before any code exists. It costs a conversation, and an agent can't have that conversation for you. It can list the questions (Agent B did), but the answer has to come from a person who can be held to it.

R2

Check the evidence behind a famous number

Learning objective

Trace a much-repeated claim back to its sources, notice which qualifications fell off along the way, and look for the evidence that tests it. The number here happens to be about requirements, but the skill applies to any statistic on a slide.

You may have seen a slide saying that a bug found in production costs 100 times more to fix than one caught during requirements. It is often used to argue for exactly this week's topic. Here is where it comes from.

The source most people mean. In January 2001, Barry Boehm and Victor Basili published a "Software Defect Reduction Top 10 List" in IEEE Computer. Item one reads: Finding and fixing a software problem after delivery is often 100 times more expensive than finding and fixing it during the requirements and design phase. The same item continues: they added the word "often" because the cost-escalation factor for small, noncritical software systems is more like 5:1 than 100:1, and good architectural practices can significantly reduce the cost-escalation factor even for large critical systems.

Older roots. That item updates Boehm's 1987 "Industrial Metrics Top 10 List". A 2017 study by Menzies and colleagues reports that nearly every citation of the effect traces back to Boehm's 1981 book Software Engineering Economics or related works, and that the book's figure drew on data from large systems of the late 1970s.

A second, weaker trail. Another version of the numbers is credited to the "IBM Systems Sciences Institute". Laurent Bossavit traced it to 1981 IBM course notes cited in a textbook, and found that the Institute was a corporate training program, not a research body. The sources checked here do not identify the project data behind those notes.

A test. Menzies, Nichols, Shull and Layman looked for the effect in 171 software projects from 2006 to 2014. They found no evidence for the delayed issue effect: resolving issues later was not consistently or substantially more expensive. Their projects all used a disciplined process with heavy inspection, which the authors discuss. It is one strong counter-example; it does not show that late fixes are always cheap.

What survives? Some late fixes are very expensive, especially in large, safety-critical systems, and especially when a decision has spread into data, contracts, and habits, as in R1. A fixed multiplier that applies to every project does not survive. The honest version of the argument for this week is smaller and stronger: an unmade decision gets made anyway, by default, and changing it later means changing everything built on it.

Try it (15 minutes, optional)

Pick a number about software you have seen on a slide or in a post this year. Find the original source. Write down the exact sentence, what it measured, when, and on whom. Which qualifications did the slide leave out?

R3

Quality from day one

Functional requirements say what the system does: join, leave, offer. Quality requirements, often called non-functional requirements, say how well and for whom: fast enough, usable by a first-timer, usable with a screen reader, careful with personal data. The international quality model ISO/IEC 25010 was revised in 2023; its nine characteristics now include safety, and what used to be called usability is now "interaction capability". You don't need the list. You need the habit of asking about quality when you ask about features. Neither agent asked about accessibility or usability; Agent B made one privacy decision on purpose, showing counts instead of names.

Both agents' pages inherit a message pop-up (a toast) from Week 1's page. Here is what the waitlist card and its toast look like against a few checks.

Accessibility. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, WCAG 2.2, became a W3C Recommendation on 5 October 2023. Its success criteria are testable statements, which makes them easy to turn into acceptance examples. Four are relevant to the waitlist:

  • 4.1.3 Status Messages (AA): status messages can be programmatically determined through role or properties such that they can be presented to the user by assistive technologies without receiving focus. The toast has role="status". But it is hidden until its text changes, and screen readers differ in how they handle that, so test it with one.
  • 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum) (AA, new in 2.2): pointer targets at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels, with some exceptions. The button is about 44 pixels tall. It passes.
  • 2.2.1 Timing Adjustable (A): for each time limit set by the content, users can turn it off, adjust it, or extend it. W3C's explanation of this criterion settles the toast question: a disappearing message is fine if there is an alternative that does not rely on a timer, some other way to find the same information. In both apps there isn't. Your number appears nowhere else, joining again is the only way to see it, and once a place frees up even that answers "reserve instead". Keep the position on the page and the criterion is met, and the usability problem is fixed too. (Whether the toast is read out at all is the separate screen-reader test in 4.1.3.)
  • 3.3.1 Error Identification (A): when an input error is detected, the item in error is identified and described in text. A missing name gets a message and the cursor moves to the field. Check it with a screen reader too.

Some problems fall between criteria. On a page with five events, every card's button says the same thing: Reserve or Join waitlist. A screen reader user who moves from button to button hears the same name five times. That is Zoe's persona card, and it is a one-line fix once somebody writes it down.

Privacy. The GDPR's data-minimisation principle says personal data must be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed. For the waitlist: store the name you need, show counts rather than names (Agent B chose this on purpose, and A does the same), and decide when the list is deleted. That last one is a decision nobody made.

Usability. "Easy to use" is not checkable. "A first-year joins a waitlist on a phone and can say her position within 60 seconds, 3 of 3 test users, no help" is. Week 9 runs real usability sessions; this week you write the criterion.

R4

Specs as input to agents

If an agent guesses where the request is silent, the obvious fix is to give it fewer silences. Several tools now build that into the workflow, under the name spec-driven development.

  • GitHub Spec Kit, released in September 2025, is an open-source toolkit that has you write a specification, then a technical plan, then a task list, before the agent implements anything. Its command names have changed since launch, so check its README rather than a blog post.
  • Kiro, announced in preview in July 2025, turns a prompt into three files, requirements.md, design.md and tasks.md, and writes acceptance criteria in EARS (Easy Approach to Requirements Syntax), a sentence pattern published in 2009 by engineers at Rolls-Royce: WHEN [condition or event] THE SYSTEM SHALL [expected behaviour].

In EARS, today's policy reads: WHEN a place frees up at an event with a waitlist, THE SYSTEM SHALL offer the place to the first person waiting for 60 minutes. That is a good requirement. Notice that it still needs the examples: what happens when the offer runs out, or when number 1 already has a place.

Two warnings. First, a spec an agent writes from a vague request contains the same guesses, now in fluent prose. It deserves the review you would give code, and its examples should come from decisions people made, not from the code they will test (Week 5 is about exactly this). Second, tools and command names in this area change month by month; the ideas underneath, examples, rules, quality requirements, named owners, are decades old. Next week you turn today's vague request into a task contract for an agent.

A made-up but realistic user, used to practise and to keep real kinds of people in mind. Not evidence.
Leading question
A question that suggests its answer ("Wouldn't it be great if…?"). Hypothetical and opinion questions have the same problem.
First come, first served · lottery · quota
Allocation policies that reward speed, luck within a window, or membership of a protected group.
Promotion, timed offer
Moving someone from a waitlist into a place, either at once or by offering the place for a fixed time.
Spec divergence test
Running the same acceptance examples against several implementations to see where their readings of a request differ.
Scope agreement
One page: in, out, rules, examples, quality, open questions, and a named owner.
WCAG 2.2
W3C's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, a set of testable success criteria at levels A, AA and AAA.
