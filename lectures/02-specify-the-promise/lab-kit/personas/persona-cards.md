# Persona cards for the discovery role-play

Print one page per card and cut along the line. Each card has a **front** that everyone sees
and a **back** that only the person playing the persona reads.

These people are **made up** for practice. Their details are plausible, not data. Real
evidence comes from the two real interviews you do for homework.

**How to play the persona.** Stay in character and answer honestly from the back of your
card. Two rules make the exercise work:

1. If you are asked about something **specific that happened** ("Tell me about the last
   time…", "What did you do then?"), answer with the facts on your card, in your own words.
2. If you are asked for an **opinion about an idea or a hypothetical** ("Would you use…?",
   "Wouldn't it be great if…?"), be friendly and agree vaguely: "Yeah, sounds good!" Don't
   volunteer the facts. That is what real people do.

## Card 1 · Priya, festival organiser

**Front.** Third-year student. Runs the escape room for the festival committee. Wants every
place filled on the night.

- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - (cut) - - - - - - - - - - - - -

**Back: only for the person playing Priya.**

Things that happened to you (reveal when asked about specific past events):
- Last year the escape room had 40 places. On the night, **7 people with a place never came**.
  You let people waiting in the corridor in at 20:10, in the order they happened to be
  standing.
- A student posted in the festival group chat that the waitlist was "rigged", because two
  people who got in late were friends of a committee member. You had no record to show it
  wasn't.
- On the Friday afternoon you answered about 30 direct messages asking "am I in?" or "can I
  have Maya's place, she's not coming?". You swapped names by hand in a spreadsheet.
- Your biggest worry is empty places at 20:00, not the order of the queue.

When asked for opinions: "A waitlist would be great, yes!"

What you care about that nobody asks: filling places **in the last hour**; a record you
can show if someone says it's unfair; fewer messages.

## Card 2 · Omar, first-year student

**Front.** First year, second month at university. Doesn't know many people yet. Wants to
go to something on the festival's first night.

- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - (cut) - - - - - - - - - - - - -

**Back: only for the person playing Omar.**

Things that happened to you (reveal when asked about specific past events):
- Booking for the silent disco opened at 12:00 on a Wednesday. You were in a chemistry lab
  until 12:25 with your phone in a locker. When you checked at 12:30 it was full.
- You checked the page four more times that day in case a place came back. You didn't know
  whether anything would ever change.
- In the end you went to the open mic, which needed no booking. Two people from your lab
  group had got silent disco places because they were refreshing at 11:59.
- You didn't complain to anyone. You assumed that's just how it works.

When asked for opinions: "Sure, I'd join a waitlist."

What you care about that nobody asks: not being locked out by your timetable; knowing
whether waiting is worth it (how many are ahead of you?).

## Card 3 · Zoe, student who uses a screen reader

**Front.** Second-year student, blind since childhood. Uses a screen reader on her laptop
and her phone every day. Books everything online.

- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - (cut) - - - - - - - - - - - - -

**Back: only for the person playing Zoe.**

Things that happened to you (reveal when asked about specific past events):
- On a cinema booking site last term, the "you're booked" message appeared and vanished
  before your screen reader finished reading the page around it. You weren't sure it had
  worked, **so you booked again and were charged twice**. Getting the refund took two weeks.
- On pages with many events you move from button to button. When ten buttons all say
  "Book", you can't tell which event each one belongs to without going back to the headings.
- You usually take a little longer to reach a button on a busy page than someone scanning
  it by eye. On "first come, first served" sales you have lost out by seconds.
- A page that tells you your place in a queue **and keeps it on screen** is the kind you
  trust.

When asked for opinions: "A waitlist? Yeah, that'd be useful."

What you care about that nobody asks: messages that stay until you've read them; buttons
whose names say which event they are for; not being penalised for being a few seconds slower.

## Card 4 · Ben, door volunteer

**Front.** Fourth-year student. Volunteers on the door: checks names off as people arrive.

- - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - (cut) - - - - - - - - - - - - -

**Back: only for the person playing Ben.**

Things that happened to you (reveal when asked about specific past events):
- Last year you worked from a printed list, because the campus Wi-Fi at the door kept
  dropping. The list was printed at 17:00, so anything that changed later wasn't on it.
- At 20:05 three people arrived saying "I was on the waitlist and someone told me to come".
  You had no list of who was waiting or in what order. You let in whoever was at the front.
- One of them had also booked the silent disco at the same time. You found out when she
  left after ten minutes.
- You want one simple rule you can apply at the door without asking anyone.

When asked for opinions: "Sounds useful, I guess."

What you care about that nobody asks: a list that works **offline** and says who is next;
a clear rule for no-shows after the start time.
